Ranking Content Decay: A Precision-at-K Approach to Editorial Refresh Prioritization

Uzair · ML Intern, FlyRank AI · Week 8 Capstone
Abstract

Content editors can't manually audit thousands of pages a week to decide what needs a refresh, so most teams rely on brittle age-and-CTR rules or gut feel. This project asks whether a supervised model can rank pages by refresh urgency better than a rule-based baseline, using only signals editors could actually know at decision time — impressions, clicks, CTR, and position from 90-day search performance. A Random Forest classifier was trained on 30,000 content assets across 32 clients, validated with a client-grouped split to prevent one client's pages from leaking between train and test. Against the same held-out set, the model reached 70% precision in its top 20 picks and 68% in its top 50, versus 20% and 34% for the rule baseline and 24.5% for random selection — roughly a 2.9× gain in how many flagged pages actually needed attention. The result is a decision-support ranking, not proof that refreshing a page recovers its traffic; that causal claim is explicitly out of scope.

01

Introduction

Search visibility decays quietly. A page can hold its position for months, then lose clicks for reasons that never show up in a simple dashboard — a title that stopped matching intent, a competitor that shipped a better answer, a scroll-depth drop that signals the content stopped landing.

Auditing that decay by hand doesn't scale. A content team with a fixed weekly capacity — five, ten, maybe twenty refreshes — needs to know which pages to open first, and a wrong call is expensive in both directions: refreshing a healthy page wastes a writer's hours, and missing a decaying one lets a competitor take the traffic before anyone notices.

This project frames that as a ranking problem: given everything knowable about a page at decision time, score it for refresh urgency, and hand editors a ranked queue instead of a spreadsheet. The specific question:

Research question
Can a model trained on observable 90-day search performance rank pages by refresh need more precisely, in the top of the queue, than a straightforward age-and-CTR rule — on a split that can't leak client-specific patterns?

The unit of analysis is one content asset (content_id) aggregated over its trailing performance window. The decision it supports: which pages a content editor opens first this week, and under what reason code.

02

Data

The working sample is 30,000 unique content assets across 32 clients, filtered from a full mid-panel release of 331,437 unique content items and 9.8M daily performance rows spanning a single calendar month (2026-03), sourced from fact_content_daily_performance. Filtering kept only pages with non-zero 90-day impressions and at least 90 days of content age, so the sample reflects established pages with a real performance history rather than newly published content still in its ramp-up period.

Fields used

Five features, chosen because an editor could know each one at decision time without hindsight:

impressions_90d clicks_90d ctr_decimal avg_position_clean sessions_90d

The label, target_needs_refresh, is a constructed proxy — not a hand-labeled ground truth. It flags pages combining high established search demand with a measured downward trend, since no team hand-tags "this page needs a refresh" at scale. That makes it a reasonable stand-in for decay, not decay itself.

Excluded, and why

  • Trend/decay source columns (trend_direction, trend_pct) — these define the label, so keeping them as features would be target leakage.
  • Client and content identifiers — used only for grouping the train/test split, never as predictive features, and never surfaced publicly.
  • Long-tail queries below Search Console's privacy threshold — Google redacts these, so low-volume signal is systematically under-reported and can't be recovered.

No client names, URLs, or query text appear anywhere in this paper or its underlying notebooks — every identifier is a hashed content_id / client_id.

03

Methodology

Baseline

The baseline is a transparent rule an editor could apply today: flag pages older than 180 days with under 2% CTR despite meaningful search views. It outputs one of four reason codes — STALE_LOW_CTR STALE_CONTENT MONITOR HEALTHY — so its picks stay auditable.

Models

Logistic Regression and a Random Forest classifier were compared against the rule. Logistic Regression first, as a sanity check — its coefficients are readable enough to catch a model leaning on a spurious signal before trusting anything more complex. Random Forest followed to capture the non-linear interactions a linear model and a hand-written rule both miss, like a page with stable impressions but quietly dropping scroll depth.

Validation design

Pages from the same client share domain authority, technical SEO setup, and baseline traffic levels. A standard row-level random split lets one client's pages sit in both train and test, so the model partly memorizes client identity rather than learning transferable decay patterns — and the reported score becomes optimistic. The fix: GroupShuffleSplit grouped by client_id (80/20, random_state=42), so every client's pages fall entirely on one side of the split. Train: 23,837 rows (35.5% positive rate). Test: 6,163 rows (24.5% positive rate).

Leakage checks

Three checks were run against the honest split: confirming the trend-source columns never entered the feature matrix; auditing correlation between each feature and the label for anything suspiciously high; and running permutation importance to confirm no single feature dominated the model's decisions.

What the naive split would have hidden
Evaluated under a naive row-level random split, the same Random Forest reported 95% precision in its top 20 — a leakage artifact from client identity crossing between train and test, not a real capability. The grouped split brought that down to the honest number reported in Results. This gap is the single most important methodological finding in this paper: the choice of split changed the headline metric by 25 points.

An honest wrinkle
The rule baseline is worse than random at K=20 (20.0% vs 24.5%) — its strict age-and-CTR cutoff filters out most of the test set, so its very top picks aren't necessarily its most confident ones. It recovers to 34% by K=50. This is reported rather than smoothed over: a simple rule can rank worse than chance at small K even when it beats chance on average.

Feature importance

Permutation importance on the honest split put impressions_90d well ahead of every other feature (importance 0.224), with ctr_decimal, sessions_90d, and clicks_90d trailing an order of magnitude lower. No single feature crossed the 0.40 threshold treated as a leakage red flag, which is consistent with the explicit leakage audit passing.

Where the model is wrong

False positives: older, high-impression pages the model flags as decaying that are actually stable — without a time-series trend feature (withheld to avoid leakage), the model reasonably but incorrectly treats "old and high-volume" as a decay signal on its own.

False negatives: pages that are genuinely declining but have relatively low raw impression volume or an unexpectedly high CTR, which reads to the model as healthy.

05

Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Not causal. This ranks pages by observed decay signal. It does not show that refreshing a flagged page recovers its traffic — that would require a before/after intervention study this project doesn't run.
  • Single 30-day window. The sample is drawn from one calendar month, so seasonal effects — a page that only looks stale outside its peak season — aren't visible to the model.
  • Search-Console privacy thresholding. Long-tail, low-volume queries are redacted by Google before this data ever arrives, understating true demand for niche pages.
  • Visibility-only signal. No on-page conversion or business-outcome data is included; "recovered clicks" is not the same as "recovered revenue."
  • Proxy label, not ground truth. target_needs_refresh is a constructed rule over observed metrics, not an editor's hand-audited judgment call, so the model is learning to match a proxy rather than true editorial need.

Everything in this paper should be read as decision support: a way to prioritize a review queue under limited editorial capacity, not an authority that overrides editorial judgment.

Ranked Recommendations

Model output maps to four archetypes, each with a reason code an editor can act on directly rather than a bare probability:

ArchetypeReason codeTriggerAction
High-value decayRC_FULL_REFRESHHigh model score, high impression volumeFull content & title refresh, top priority
Stale, low CTRSTALE_LOW_CTRAged content, weak click-through despite visibilityRewrite title & meta first
Stale contentSTALE_CONTENTAged content, healthier CTRUpdate facts & sources, lighter touch

At fixed editorial capacity, prioritizing this ranked queue over an unordered review list yields roughly a 2.9× efficiency gain — the share of reviewed pages that actually needed attention, versus reviewing in no particular order.

Human sign-off, by design

Of the 30,000-page queue, 15,086 pages (just over half) are flagged for mandatory human review before any action — high-impression or high-priority pages where an automated call carries too much downside. The remaining 14,914 fall into standard monitoring.

Strict automation ban
This system never automatically deletes or prunes content, and never auto-publishes AI-generated rewrites without human review. It ranks and recommends; a person decides.

Monitoring & retrain triggers

Scheduled retraining every 90 days on refreshed rolling performance data, plus an early trigger: if monthly manual audits show rolling Precision@20 drop below 50%, retraining is pulled forward rather than waiting for the quarterly cycle.
